Why bond prices move opposite to yields

Build the intuition with a small fixed-rate bond rather than memorising the rule.

On this page
UnderstandExplorePracticeExplain itKey takeawaysSources

UNDERSTAND

A bond is a set of promised cash flows

A simple fixed-rate bond promises coupon payments and repayment of face value at maturity. Its market price depends on what investors are willing to pay today for those future cash flows.

If market yields rise, the same fixed cash flows become less attractive relative to newly available returns. The price of the existing bond therefore has to fall to offer a competitive yield. If market yields fall, the reverse happens.

The direction to remember

For a plain fixed-rate bond, yield ↑ → price ↓ and yield ↓ → price ↑, all else equal.

PRACTICE

Check the intuition

A bond pays a fixed 2% coupon. New comparable bonds now offer higher yields. What normally happens to the old bond’s price?

Why does price adjust?

EXPLAIN IT

60 second interview practice

“Why do bond prices generally fall when interest rates rise?”

  1. An existing fixed-rate bond has predetermined cash flows.
  2. Higher market yields make those cash flows less attractive.
  3. The bond price falls until its effective return is competitive.
  4. Longer maturity and lower coupon often imply greater sensitivity, all else equal.

KEY TAKEAWAYS

Keep the mechanism

  • Bond value = present value of future cash flows.
  • Discount rate/yield up → present value down.
  • Coupon and yield are not the same thing.
  • Maturity affects rate sensitivity.
